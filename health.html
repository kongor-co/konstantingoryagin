Health

Health, movement and feeling good in my body

I care a lot about my health, but I am not trying to become a professional athlete, bodybuilder or health optimizer.

For me, being healthy means having enough energy to live an active life, moving without pain, feeling comfortable in my body, recovering well, thinking clearly and being able to do the things I want to do without my body becoming the limitation.

I care about longevity, strength, mobility, appearance, mental clarity, confidence, sexual health and remaining independent as I get older.

But I do not want to maximize any of those things at the expense of everything else.

My approach is built around balance.

I would rather make many small decisions that I can maintain for years than follow an extreme routine for three months.

Health should support my life, not become my life.

I optimize for life, not numbers

I deliberately do not have targets for body fat, muscle mass, lifting numbers, running pace or calories.

I own a fitness tracker, but mostly use it for notifications and occasionally for steps. I have experimented with sleep tracking as well, but I often find that a device can tell me I slept badly while I wake up feeling rested, or the opposite.

That has made me skeptical of turning health into a dashboard.

Numbers can be useful, especially when there is a medical reason to measure something, but I do not want to accidentally optimize for the metric instead of what the metric is supposed to represent.

My primary feedback system is much less sophisticated:

How do I feel? How do I move? How do I look? Do I have energy? Am I sleeping well? Does anything hurt?

I want to pay attention to my body rather than outsource that awareness entirely to a device.

Movement every day

Most mornings start with 20 to 30 minutes of relatively light movement.

I work gradually through my body, from my neck and shoulders down to my ankles. I move and stretch the joints and usually mix in some bodyweight exercises such as squats, push-ups, pull-ups, sit-ups and abdominal work.

It is not intended to be a hard workout.

The purpose is simply to move.

Because I work from home and spend much of the day sitting at a computer, this has become particularly important to me. I do not want exercise to be something that happens three times a week while the remaining waking hours are almost completely sedentary.

If I get 10 or 15 minutes between meetings, I might pick up dumbbells, do some pull-ups or push-ups, train my abs, hang from the rings or simply stretch.

My minimum workout

When time or motivation is limited, I fall back to four basic movements:

Pull-ups. Push-ups. Squats. Abdominal work.

I can do some version of them almost anywhere.

How I train

During a normal week, I try to cover my shoulders, arms, chest, back, core and legs.

There is no strict schedule. I care more about making sure that nothing is consistently forgotten.

Most training happens at home in sessions of around 30 to 40 minutes. Once or twice a week I usually go to a commercial gym, where I train for roughly an hour to an hour and a half. I also try to include a cardio-focused session such as fitboxing.

My favorite exercises are relatively simple: pull-ups, push-ups, squats, kettlebell and dumbbell movements, gymnastic rings and, increasingly, handstands.

When strength training, movement quality matters more to me than how impressive the weight looks.

I want a weight that requires noticeable effort while still allowing me to maintain good posture and control.

With exercises that train one side at a time, I normally start with my weaker left side and then match the number of repetitions with the stronger side.

I am not trying to discover the maximum amount of weight I can move once.

I am trying to build a body that works well.

Mobility

Mobility is one of the main reasons I train.

I have read many stories about people reaching an older age and struggling with basic movements: bending down, putting on socks, getting dressed or moving comfortably through everyday life.

I do not want that to become normal for me.

My goal is not extreme flexibility. It is much simpler:

I want to keep being able to move without pain.

Handstands: a skill in progress

I recently started practicing handstands.

I cannot yet hold one properly without a wall, and part of the challenge is simply becoming comfortable being upside down.

My biggest obstacle is psychological rather than physical: I am still afraid of falling badly and hurting my neck or back.

For now, I practice cautiously next to a wall and treat it primarily as a movement skill I would like to learn.

It is a useful reminder that even as an adult there are physical skills where I can still be a complete beginner.

Cardio is one of my weaker areas

I do not particularly enjoy running, and pretending otherwise would not make this page more useful.

I walk quite a lot. On normal days I try to get outside in the evening and reach at least around 6,000 steps. When travelling on weekends, 15 to 20 kilometres of walking in a day is fairly normal.

I enjoy hiking, previously cycled regularly, sometimes use the rowing machine at the gym and use fitboxing for more intense cardiovascular work.

Compared with strength and mobility, however, cardio is an area where I could be more deliberate.

This page describes what I actually do, not the perfect routine I wish I followed.

Working at a desk

One of the health problems I think about most is not training.

It is sitting.

A workout in the morning does not change the fact that I may then spend six or seven hours at a desk.

So I try to interrupt sitting whenever possible.

I move between meetings. I hang from gymnastic rings to stretch and decompress. I use a gym ball and foam roller for my back and neck. I occasionally use a massage gun, and I have small massage devices around my desk.

The most useful lesson for me has been that movement does not need to wait for a workout.

Five minutes counts. Ten minutes counts. Getting up and moving between two meetings counts.

If you work at a desk

These are personal habits that have helped me, not medical advice:

  • Keep water within reach.
  • Move for 5 to 10 minutes every 1 to 2 hours.
  • Set up the chair, desk and screen so the position feels natural.
  • Pay attention to head and neck position.
  • Avoid spending the entire day in exactly the same position.
  • Use short breaks for movement rather than automatically opening another screen.

Food: mostly simple, with room for enjoyment

My nutrition philosophy is also based around balance rather than perfection.

I usually do not eat early in the morning. I start the day with water and normally have my first proper meal around noon.

That meal often includes fruit, bread and a source of protein. During the day I might snack on raw fruit or vegetables, yogurt, nuts, a sandwich or something protein-rich.

My larger cooked meal is normally in the evening and is relatively simple: rice, potatoes or noodles, vegetables or salad, plus meat or another protein source.

I generally try to eat more fruits, vegetables, nuts, yogurt and protein and less added sugar, heavily processed food, excessive oils, butter, white bread and liquid calories.

Water is my default drink.

I do not drink soda, do not drink coffee and drink very little alcohol.

But I also eat dessert.

I eat pizza.

I go to restaurants.

Approximately 80 percent good decisions and 20 percent flexibility feels considerably more sustainable to me than trying to eat perfectly and feeling guilty whenever reality interferes.

My basic food principles

  • Make water the default drink.
  • Eat fruits and vegetables regularly.
  • Get enough protein.
  • Prefer recognizable, relatively minimally processed foods.
  • Keep added sugar limited.
  • Avoid drinking calories by default.
  • Cook simple meals.
  • Stop eating before feeling completely full.
  • Do not turn imperfect meals into guilt.
  • Leave room for food that exists simply because it is enjoyable.

Supplements

I prefer food to be the foundation of my nutrition.

I also use protein and creatine and occasionally take some nutritional supplements.

I do not view supplements as shortcuts and I am still refining how I approach them.

This is one area where I prefer to stay cautious rather than continuously add new products because they are fashionable.

Alcohol

I barely drink alcohol.

There is no great discipline involved because I simply do not particularly like it.

I can have fun without it, and I dislike the dehydration, worse sleep and weak feeling that can follow drinking.

There are occasions where I enjoy the social ritual: a glass of wine during a romantic dinner or a beer while talking about life with a friend.

For me that is enough.

Again, balance works better than ideology.

Sleep and recovery

I have become more interested in sleep as I have gotten older.

I normally go to bed when I feel tired, somewhere between roughly 22:00 and midnight. Whenever my schedule allows it, I prefer waking naturally instead of using an alarm.

Before sleeping I like to read and try to reduce screen time.

Late meals and late workouts noticeably interfere with my sleep, so I prefer to finish both a few hours before bed.

When possible, I also sleep with some fresh air coming through the window.

Travel is more difficult because I do not sleep particularly well in unfamiliar places. After travelling for a while, I can feel a growing sleep deficit and appreciate returning home.

I have used sleep tracking, but I treat the numbers as secondary information. How rested and functional I feel the next day matters more to me than whether an app gives the night an impressive score.

Recovery does not always mean doing more

Recovery for me can mean sleep, sauna, lighter training, stretching, spending time outdoors, reading or simply doing nothing.

I do not believe in pushing through every signal the body sends me.

If I feel unusually tired, I reduce the intensity, train something else or rest.

Sometimes resting is the training.

Mental health is health

I do not separate mental and physical health very cleanly.

Stress affects my sleep. Poor sleep affects my training. Mental tension changes how my body feels. Exercise changes my mood.

For me, physical health without mental health does not work.

Reading helps. Exercise helps. Nature helps. Talking helps. Therapy can help. Solitude helps. Intimacy helps. Sometimes doing absolutely nothing helps.

I am naturally quite impulsive and can overthink things, so maintaining inner calm requires active work from me.

I increasingly consider protecting that calm part of taking care of my health rather than something separate from it.

Nature

Nature is particularly effective for me.

Even a short walk through the fields and watching the sunset can noticeably change my mood.

I like being near water and sometimes even work from there.

I enjoy sunshine and notice that long, dark winter periods affect my mood.

I probably still spend less time outside than I would like.

One version of a future home I often imagine is very simple: being able to sit inside while looking directly into nature.

What I don't optimize

I do not want the lowest possible body-fat percentage.

I do not need the biggest muscles.

I do not care about running a marathon.

I do not need perfect nutrition.

I do not need every minute of sleep quantified.

I do not need every workout tracked.

I do not want my life organized around health.

I want my health to support my life.

Things I still struggle with

  • Sitting for too long
  • Sweets
  • Cardio consistency
  • Stress
  • Procrastination
  • Overthinking
  • Staying outside the comfort zone
  • Maintaining habits when routines change

Knowing what is healthy does not automatically make it easy to do.

Anything that requires consistency can still break surprisingly quickly. I use tools and routines to help myself, and I am even building an app partly around this problem, but I still have days when comfort wins.

I think that is more realistic than pretending healthy habits eventually require no effort.

What I want my body to do at 70

When I think about longevity, I do not primarily imagine a number of years.

I imagine capability.

At 50, 60 or 70, I want to walk long distances, hike, climb ten floors without being completely exhausted, play football, basketball or volleyball, do push-ups and squats, lift my own body weight and pick up the people I love.

I want to be able to put my socks on without help.

I want movement to remain normal.

That is a much more meaningful definition of longevity to me than simply living for as many years as possible.

The long-term test

I want to still be able to:

  • Walk long distances
  • Climb stairs comfortably
  • Hike
  • Play sports
  • Squat
  • Do push-ups
  • Do pull-ups
  • Lift and carry things
  • Move through a full range of motion
  • Dress independently
  • Feel comfortable in my body

What I'm working on now

My health goals right now are intentionally not very dramatic.

I want to stay healthy, keep moving every day, improve my cardio, continue learning the handstand and give my nervous system enough time and space to become calmer.

I am particularly focused on maintaining routines when life around me changes.

The goal is not a transformation.

The goal is continuity.

A simple reminder

My current health philosophy

Move every day.

Eat mostly simple food.

Protect sleep and mental peace.

Pay attention to your body without turning your life into a spreadsheet.

Do not overdo it. Many small steps that survive are more useful than one enormous jump that breaks something along the way.

A note on health information

This page documents my personal routines, experiences and current thinking. It is not medical, nutritional or training advice.

What works for me may not be appropriate for someone else, and my own views will probably continue to change as I learn more.

For medical questions, symptoms, supplements or significant changes to exercise or nutrition, professional advice is more appropriate than copying somebody's personal website.
